**Straight ahead** means drawing frame 1, then frame 2 from frame 1, and so on. The result is spontaneous and fluid (fire, smoke, water, hair), but hard to control.
**Pose to pose** means drawing the key poses first, then filling in the [in-betweens](../../glossary/#in-betweens) (the frames between them). The result is planned and quick to retime, which suits acting and interfaces.

The script draws each flame frame by nudging the one before, and `.toon-sprite` plays them with `steps()`, so there are no in-betweens. `toon.css` has no pose-to-pose preset because every `@keyframes` block already is one: you write the key poses and the browser draws the rest.

## Straight ahead vs pose to pose in Canvas

`straightAhead` runs a step function at a fixed frame rate, and each step moves the smoke on from where the last one left it. `poses` takes key poses on a timeline from 0 to 1, and each key's `ease` shapes the move into that pose. `samplePoses` does the same interpolation as a plain function, for scrubbing or ghost frames.

## Straight ahead vs pose to pose: tuning tips

- **Mix the two approaches.** Pose the main body, then simulate hair, cloth and tails straight ahead on top (see [follow-through](../follow-through/)).
- **Use a lower frame rate for a hand-made feel.** Straight-ahead motion at 12 or 24 fps looks more drawn than at 60 fps.
- **Retime by moving keys, not frames.** With pose to pose, a slower wave means changing a few `at` values. The in-betweens follow on their own.
- **Show the key poses while you work.** Drawing them faintly behind the figure, as the Canvas snippet does, shows at a glance whether the in-betweens travel where you meant.

The demo's wave also shows [anticipation](../anticipation/) (the arm dips before it rises) and [exaggeration](../exaggeration/) (it overshoots the top pose).

For every option, see the [Canvas timing and sequencing functions](../../api/canvas/#timing-and-sequencing) and the [CSS sprite preset](../../api/css/#effects).
